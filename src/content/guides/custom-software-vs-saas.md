Buy off-the-shelf software when a product already does 80 per cent of what you need and your process is not how you win customers. Build custom software when the workflow is your competitive edge, when per-seat fees are growing faster than your business, or when nothing on the market fits the way you actually operate.

That is the short answer. The longer one matters because both mistakes are common and expensive: businesses build things they could have bought for a few hundred dollars a month, and businesses bend their operations around a SaaS tool for years because a build felt too risky. Below is the framework we use when a client asks, with real numbers.

## When should you buy SaaS instead of building?

SaaS wins in more situations than most founders expect. Buy when:

- **The problem is generic.** Email, accounting, payroll, help desk, document signing, video calls and standard CRM are solved problems with mature products. Your version will not be better, only yours.
- **You are still learning your process.** A young company changes how it works every quarter. SaaS lets you change tools; custom software makes every change a project.
- **Headcount is small.** Per-seat pricing hurts at 200 users and is trivial at 8.
- **The vendor's roadmap is your roadmap.** If the product's new features are the ones you would have asked for, you are paying for a development team you could not afford.
- **Compliance is included.** Payment handling, tax rules and data protection certifications are worth buying rather than building.

The cost of choosing SaaS is not only the subscription. It is the parts of your process that do not fit and the workarounds your team invents: spreadsheets on the side, double entry between tools, and reports assembled by hand every Monday. When those workarounds start to look like a full-time job, the calculation changes.

## When does custom software win?

Build when one or more of these is true:

- **The workflow is your advantage.** If the way you quote, dispatch, onboard or fulfil is the reason customers choose you, software that makes it faster and more consistent is an investment in the business itself, not an overhead.
- **Nothing fits.** You have evaluated three or four products and each one forces a compromise that costs real time or money. This is common in logistics, manufacturing, healthcare operations and any business that combines physical operations with customer service.
- **The SaaS bill is growing faster than revenue.** Per-seat pricing plus add-ons plus the integration tool you needed to make it work can exceed what a purpose-built system would cost to own.
- **You need to connect several systems.** When the value is in the joins between your tools, a custom integration layer is often the smallest build that solves the whole problem.
- **Data ownership and control matter.** Regulated data, unusual retention rules or the need to run in a particular country's infrastructure can rule out most SaaS.
- **Customers will use it.** A customer-facing portal that looks and behaves like your brand is a product, and products are worth building well.

## What does each option really cost over three years?

Let us take a concrete example: a CRM for a 25-person sales and service team in India. We will use published Zoho CRM prices as the SaaS option because they are public and typical of the category. According to [Zoho's Indian pricing page](https://www.zoho.com/crm/zohocrm-pricing.html), the Standard plan is ₹800 per user per month and the Enterprise plan is ₹2,400 per user per month. For the custom option we use our blended rate of ₹2,500 per hour (about $30) and a typical build of 400 to 800 hours for a focused CRM with the workflows this team actually uses, plus a common planning figure of 15 to 20 per cent of the build cost per year for hosting, maintenance and small changes.

| Option | Year 1 | Years 2 and 3 | Three-year total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zoho CRM Standard, 25 users | ₹2,40,000 | ₹4,80,000 | ₹7,20,000 (about $8,600) |
| Zoho CRM Enterprise, 25 users | ₹7,20,000 | ₹14,40,000 | ₹21,60,000 (about $26,000) |
| Custom CRM, 400 hours build | ₹10,00,000 build + ₹2,00,000 maintenance | ₹4,00,000 | ₹16,00,000 (about $19,000) |
| Custom CRM, 800 hours build | ₹20,00,000 build + ₹4,00,000 maintenance | ₹8,00,000 | ₹32,00,000 (about $38,000) |

Currency conversions are approximate. The pattern is what matters.

If the Standard plan does what you need, buying is clearly cheaper and you should buy. If you need the Enterprise tier for its workflow rules and customisation, a focused custom CRM costs about the same over three years and less after that, because the subscription keeps going while the build is paid for. If you need something large and unusual, the custom option costs more and you should be sure the fit is worth it.

Three factors tilt this table in practice. Headcount growth makes SaaS more expensive every year and leaves custom cost roughly flat. Integrations you would have to buy separately for SaaS, whether through an automation platform or the vendor's own add-ons, are usually included in a custom build. And a custom system does exactly what your process needs, so the hidden cost of workarounds disappears.

## The middle path: buy the platform, build the edges

The choice is rarely all or nothing, and the best answer for most businesses we work with is a combination.

Keep the SaaS tool as the system of record for what it does well, then build the pieces around it that the vendor will never build for you. Common examples:

- A **customer portal** that reads from your CRM, ERP or transport system and gives clients self-service tracking, documents and invoices under your brand.
- An **integration layer** that keeps your CRM, accounting package and operations tool in sync so data is entered once. The CRM integration and third-party API integration pages give typical effort for this kind of work.
- An **automation** that does the step the SaaS workflow builder cannot: reading documents, calculating a quote from your rate rules, or routing work by a logic the vendor does not support. Increasingly this is where [AI features](/services/ai-solutions) earn their keep.
- A **reporting layer** that pulls from several tools into one dashboard, instead of paying for the top SaaS tier just to get its reporting module.

This approach costs a fraction of a full custom build, keeps the vendor's ongoing development working for you, and lets you replace individual tools later without rebuilding everything.

## Signs you have outgrown your SaaS

If several of these sound familiar, the build conversation is worth having.

- Your team maintains spreadsheets that duplicate data from the tool because the tool cannot show it the way they need.
- You pay for an automation platform, a reporting add-on and two integrations just to make the core product usable.
- New staff take weeks to learn the workarounds rather than the work.
- Customers ask for something simple, such as seeing their own order status, and the honest answer is that the tool does not do that.
- You have been told a feature is on the roadmap for more than a year.
- The subscription has become one of your largest recurring costs and is growing with headcount rather than with value.

## Risks of building, and how to manage them

Custom software fails for predictable reasons, and each has a known countermeasure.

**Scope grows.** Fix the first release in writing, ship it to real users, and let usage decide the second release. A smaller version one that people use beats a larger one that is still being built.

**The builder disappears.** Own the code repository, hosting, domain and every third-party account from day one. Ask for documentation and a handover, even if you plan to keep working with the same people.

**Nobody owns it after launch.** Software needs patching and small changes for as long as it is in use. Budget 15 to 20 per cent of the build cost per year and give someone, in-house or a part-time developer, clear responsibility.

**It is built to be clever rather than boring.** Insist on mainstream languages, frameworks and hosting so that any competent developer can pick it up later.

**The estimate was a guess.** Get a written scope with tiers and hours, not a single number. The guide on [custom web app costs](/guides/custom-web-app-development-cost) explains what a credible estimate contains.

## A simple decision checklist

Answer these honestly and the decision usually makes itself.

1. Does a well-known product do at least 80 per cent of this today? If yes, buy, and revisit in a year.
2. Is this process a reason customers choose us, or just something we have to do? Build only for the former.
3. What do the workarounds cost us in hours per week? Multiply by 50 weeks and by a loaded hourly cost.
4. What will the SaaS bill be in three years at our expected headcount, including add-ons?
5. Can the SaaS tool export our data and expose an API? If not, avoid building your operation around it.
6. Who will own the custom system after launch, and what is their budget?
7. Is there a middle path: keep the tool and build the edges?
