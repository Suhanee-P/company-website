Hire a part-time developer when you have steady work that does not fill a full week, a freelancer for a well-defined short project, and an agency when you need several skills at once and want someone else to manage delivery. The right choice depends on cost, continuity and how much management you can take on, not on the label.

Most founders and operations leaders end up choosing between these options with incomplete information, usually after one bad experience. This guide compares them on the things that matter in practice, puts real numbers on each, and lists the questions to ask before you sign anything.

## What is the difference between a part-time developer, a freelancer and an agency?

The terms overlap, so it helps to define them by how the engagement works rather than by who is doing it.

A **freelancer** is an individual you hire for a project or a task. They quote per project or per hour, work alone or with people they bring in, and typically move on when the work is done.

A **part-time or fractional developer** is an individual or a small team that works for you on a recurring basis, usually a fixed number of hours per week or per month. The point is continuity: the same person knows your systems and picks up whatever is next without a new discovery phase each time.

An **agency** is a company that sells a team. You get a project manager, one or more developers, a designer and testing, with the agency responsible for delivering the agreed scope. Rates are higher because you are paying for that overhead and for cover when someone is unavailable.

A **full-time hire** is an employee. You get their whole week and full control over priorities, and you take on salary, benefits, management and the risk that the work does not fill the week.

## How much does each option cost?

Rates vary a great deal by region and seniority, so treat these as planning figures. The Aresyn figures use our blended rate of $30 per hour (about ₹2,500 per hour) for a senior India-based team.

| Option | Typical cost | What it buys | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freelancer (India, senior) | $20 to $50 per hour, or a fixed project price | One person, one project, limited cover | Defined projects under three months |
| Freelancer (US or UK, senior) | $75 to $150 per hour | Same, in your time zone | Short specialist work, local presence |
| Part-time developer (senior, India-based) | About $2,600 a month for 20 hours a week at $30 per hour | Continuity, a person who knows your systems | Ongoing improvements, integrations, support |
| Agency (India) | $25 to $60 per hour, usually quoted per project | Managed team, design and testing included | Larger builds with several skills needed |
| Agency (US or UK) | $100 to $200 per hour | Managed team in your market | Enterprise projects, regulated industries |
| Full-time engineer (US) | $135,623 average base salary per year according to [Indeed](https://www.indeed.com/career/software-engineer/salaries), August 2026, plus benefits and equipment | Full week, full control | Software is the product and the work never stops |
| Full-time engineer (UK) | £50,167 average base salary per year according to [Indeed](https://uk.indeed.com/career/software-engineer/salaries), August 2026, plus employer costs | Same | Same |
| Full-time engineer (India) | ₹8,88,879 average base salary per year according to [Indeed](https://in.indeed.com/career/software-engineer/salaries), August 2026 | Same, at local cost | Businesses with an Indian office and daily engineering needs |

Two things stand out. First, a senior part-time developer for half a week costs roughly a quarter of a US full-time salary, before the employer costs that typically add 20 to 30 per cent on top of base pay. Second, agency and freelancer rates in India overlap, so the choice between them is about management and cover, not price.

Hourly rate is also the wrong thing to optimise. A senior developer who has built a customer portal before will finish it in fewer hours, with fewer defects, than a cheaper developer learning on your budget. Compare total quoted price for a defined scope, and ask what happens to the price if the scope changes.

## When is a part-time developer the right choice?

Part-time works when the software matters to your business but is not the business itself. Typical situations:

- You have an internal tool, a customer portal or an ecommerce site that needs a steady flow of small improvements, and nobody in-house to make them.
- You have three or four systems that should talk to each other and keep needing new integrations as you add suppliers, channels or customers.
- You built version one with an agency or a freelancer and now need someone to own it: fix bugs, keep dependencies patched, add the next feature.
- You want a senior technical person you can call before you sign a software contract, without paying for a chief technology officer.

The main risk is under-using the hours. Agree a simple backlog, review it weekly and let the developer pull the next item, rather than waiting until you have a big request.

## When is a freelancer the right choice?

A freelancer suits work that is clearly bounded and short: a website, a data migration, a specific integration, a prototype to test an idea. The engagement is cheap to start and easy to end.

The risks are continuity and breadth. If the freelancer leaves, becomes unavailable or moves to a full-time job, you inherit code that nobody else understands. And a single person is rarely strong at design, development, testing and infrastructure all at once, so something in the project will get less attention than it deserves.

Reduce those risks by owning every account, paying against delivered milestones, and asking for a short written handover at the end even if you expect to keep working together.

## When is an agency the right choice?

An agency makes sense when the project needs more than one skill at the same time, when you do not have the time or experience to manage developers, or when the timeline needs several people working in parallel. A customer platform with design, web, mobile and integrations is agency-shaped work. So is anything with a fixed launch date tied to a contract or an event.

You pay for that with higher rates and, sometimes, with distance. Ask who will actually work on your project and whether you can talk to them directly. Some agencies put a project manager between you and the people writing the code, which slows every decision. Weekly demos of working software, not slide decks, are the fix.

## What about a full-time hire?

Hire full-time when software is central to what you sell and the engineering work genuinely never stops. Then the control, availability and accumulated knowledge are worth the salary, the recruiting time and the management.

Hire cautiously if the work is a series of projects with gaps in between. A full-time developer with nothing pressing to do either builds things you did not need or leaves. Many businesses are better served by a part-time developer for the steady work plus a project team for the occasional larger build.

## The six questions to ask before you sign

Ask every option the same questions and compare the answers.

1. **Who will actually do the work, and can I speak to them?** Names, not roles.
2. **What have you built that looks like this?** Ask to see it running, and ask what went wrong on that project.
3. **What exactly is in scope, and what happens when I ask for something extra?** You want a written scope and a clear process for changes.
4. **How will I see progress?** Weekly demos of working software should be the answer.
5. **Who owns the code, the accounts and the data?** You should, from the first day.
6. **What happens after launch?** Support terms, response times and the cost of ongoing work.

Vague answers to any of these are a better predictor of trouble than the hourly rate.

## How to reduce the risk whichever option you choose

The same practices protect you in every model.

Fix scope in writing before work starts. Even for a part-time engagement, a one-page description of the first month's priorities avoids a slow start. The guide on [writing a software project brief](/guides/how-to-write-a-software-project-brief) covers what to include.

Own your infrastructure. The code repository, hosting account, domain, app store accounts and third-party services should be in your company's name, with your developer added as a collaborator. This is the single most effective protection against a bad ending.

Pay against milestones or short cycles, not long blocks of time in advance. Two-week cycles with a demo at the end keep everyone honest.

Ask for boring documentation: how to deploy, where the environment variables live, which services the system depends on. Two pages are enough and they make the next developer's first week productive instead of archaeological.
